Rabbit Hole #2: The Story of Pokémon Go

The future of AI data collection is hidden in consumer apps

Nancy TranMar 24, 2026Read on Substack

If you’ve been paying any attention to tech lately, you’ve probably noticed that there’s a LOT happening in the physical AI space right now. Like a lot a lot. Tesla’s Optimus is folding laundry in real homes. Figure’s robot is working shifts on a BMW factory floor. Physical Intelligence, a startup founded eighteen months ago, already has a model that can do your dishes, fold clothes, and pack boxes, all in one brain across different robot bodies. Boston Dynamics’ Atlas dropped its hydraulics and went fully electric. When you watch videos of these robots doing their thing, you notice how they have this almost human-like spatial awareness. They can look at a cluttered counter and understand what’s there, what matters, and what to do next.

About 10 days ago, MIT Technology Review published an article that talked about how one lab approached training models to give robots exactly this kind of spatial awareness. The article itself was interesting, but what really caught my attention was HOW they did it. They did it using Pokémon Go. Yes, the game where we go around catching Pokémon, the game that got all of us walking around getting in our 10,000 steps when our mothers couldn’t do the same with all forms of “creative” nudging. Pokémon Go had somehow gotten millions of people to not only walk, and in doing so, they also happened to be contributing data that now trains these models.

What the MIT Article Actually Said

So here’s what the article talked about. Niantic, the company behind Pokémon Go, spun out an AI company called Niantic Spatial. And what Niantic Spatial is doing is repurposing the massive trove of crowdsourced images from Pokémon Go players to help delivery robots navigate city streets with centimeter-level precision.

GPS is notoriously unreliable in dense urban areas. Signals bounce off skyscrapers and can be off by 50 meters or more. Delivery robots can’t afford to get lost, and a 50-meter error margin is a lot. With this margin of error, robots are not going to arrive at their supposed destination. In the robotics world, last-mile delivery is important. Translation: arriving “roughly around 50 meters” from where they’re supposed to go won’t make the cut.

Niantic solved this by collecting real-world images of places from many people. Over the years, hundreds of millions of Pokémon Go and Ingress players unknowingly built an enormous visual dataset, 30 billion images of urban landmarks, each tagged with highly accurate location metadata (position, orientation, movement, all of it). Niantic Spatial trained a visual positioning model on this data that can determine a robot’s exact location to within a few centimeters, just by looking at nearby buildings and landmarks.

As the first real-world test, Niantic Spatial partnered with Coco Robotics, which operates about 1,000 sidewalk delivery robots in cities such as Los Angeles, Chicago, and Helsinki. Coco’s robots carry things like pizzas and groceries and will now use Niantic Spatial’s model alongside GPS to navigate more reliably, pulling up precisely to restaurant pickup spots and customer doorsteps.

The bigger vision from Niantic Spatial’s leaders is that this is the foundation of a “living map”, a hyper-detailed, constantly updated digital replica of the real world designed not just for humans but for machines. As more robots contribute data, the maps become richer and more useful for AI systems that need to understand and interact with physical environments.

Reading that article was kind of a holy sh*t moment for me. How did Pokémon Go, a game I witnessed my whole friend group play in high school (and honestly even in college), end up being a part of the physical AI puzzle?

The History of Pokémon Go

Pokémon Go was developed by Niantic, co-founded by John Hanke. Before Niantic, John Hanke co-founded another startup, Keyhole, in 2001. Keyhole Inc. set out to build 3D maps of Earth from satellite images. In 2004, Google acquired Keyhole, and that became Google Earth. From 2005 to 2010, Hanke led the development of Google Maps and Google Street View. In 2010, Hanke founded Niantic Labs, which at the time was just an internal startup inside of Google. It wasn’t until 2015 that Niantic spun out of Google independently and became the Niantic we know today. In addition to Google, Nintendo and the Pokémon Company also invested in Niantic and licensed the IP to them (what a brilliant idea)!

Because in 2016, when Niantic launched Pokémon Go, Nintendo, which by then had minimal operational involvement in the game, benefited greatly. Nintendo’s stock actually DOUBLED in nine days after Pokémon Go launched. People saw Pokémon Go and bought Nintendo stock en masse, not fully understanding that Niantic and Nintendo/The Pokémon Company had minimal involvement with each other. Nintendo continued to benefit mostly through the IP licensing revenue from the Pokémon Company’s stake.

Sorry, I sidetracked, just had to mention that one fun fact. But we are back.

So, Pokémon Go was launched in 2016. I was in high school then and distinctly remember how the world quite literally lost it. People were on it 24/7. Within 48 hours, Pokémon Go crashed the App Store. At its peak, there were 45 million daily active users. I didn’t know this, but at the time, it was the first game ever to top both the App Store and Google Play simultaneously. Pokémon Go got $200M in revenue in its first month. For reference, Clash Royale, one of the biggest mobile games at the time, made about $125M in its first 30 days. Pokémon Go nearly doubled it.

And the secret to keeping users constantly engaged, and therefore constantly generating data, was the constant influx of new events and shiny Pokémon releases. Gen 2 Pokémon in 2017. The first shiny Pokémon during the Water Festival. Raid Battles and Legendary Pokémon at GO Fest. Community Days. Team GO Rocket and Shadow Pokémon. Remote Raid Passes during COVID. Mega Evolution. Shadow Raids. Every few weeks, something new to pull players back in. You get the idea. Everyone loves going around catching Pokémon.

The Hidden Data Machine

Most people know Pokémon Go as the app that people use to travel around and catch Pokémon. The interesting thing about this app is that it wasn’t just an entertaining app; it also had an underlying data infrastructure that allowed game developers to collect geographic data as users played.

The fact that the game was designed as a data collection infrastructure is brilliant. Because not only does it make the user experience 10x more interesting, it makes the data incredibly useful.

Looking back in history, this is not the first piece of software Niantic designed as a data-collection infrastructure. In 2013, they launched an app called Ingress, a sci-fi AR game where players had to submit photos and GPS coordinates of interesting real-world locations. Ingress became the database of PokéStops and Gyms in Pokémon Go. Niantic effectively crowdsourced a global landmark map before the main game even launched.

And so, when Niantic launched Pokémon Go itself, it continued to incorporate the data-collection crowdsourcing component into the app. The game and the data product were the same product. The user just didn’t know it.

In 2019, Niantic released a killer feature: the AR scanning feature that lets you scan at PokéStops. Translation: you show up to your PokéStop and use your phone to film the location from multiple angles. Niantic’s software then stitches the video into a 3D mesh of that location.

This allows Niantic to create photogrammetry at a civilization scale: multiple players, multiple cities, all building towards one 3D map. What a great way to DELEGATE! WOW! Thank you everyone for doing so much work, right? Teamwork makes the dream work.

What’s special about this feature is that it allows multi-angle captures (unlike Google Street View, which shows only one angle at a time), and because multiple people take part, the scans can be repeated over time. This means you get pretty updated data fairly frequently.

What I’m curious about, and still not sure about, is whether this was a bug or a feature. Did Niantic plan for this to be a deliberate data collection infrastructure from day one, or did they just find another way to use the data years later, after it had all been collected?

What Problem Did Pokémon Go Actually Solve?

OK, so now the question is, how exactly did Pokémon Go get itself to this moment where it’s involved in the physical AI world? What problem did it exactly solve?

Localization. Ladies and gents, the answer is localization! It’s solving the question of when a machine looks at the world through a camera and asks, “Where exactly am I?”

GPS gives you a rough answer, usually within 5 to 50 meters, depending on conditions. That’s fine for navigation but catastrophic for robots. A 10-meter error means a delivery robot stops in the middle of the road instead of at your door. A humanoid robot reaches for a cup and knocks over a glass. The margin for error in the physical world needs to be extremely small, if possible, essentially zero.

So the question Niantic was really answering is: can a machine figure out exactly where it is and which direction it’s facing, just by looking at what’s in front of it? The same way you’d recognize you’re standing outside a specific coffee shop because you can see the green awning, the brick wall, and the fire hydrant on the left.

This is called Visual Positioning. And solving it requires a very specific kind of data.

Here’s what makes the Pokémon Go data so special. Every time a player’s phone captured an image, it didn’t just record what the camera saw. It recorded a village worth of context around that image: exact GPS coordinates at the moment of capture, which direction the phone was facing, tilt of the phone (was it held up, down, angled?), whether it was moving, walking speed, direction of movement, timestamp (time of day, date, season), device altitude (how high above ground).

Think of it this way. Imagine you have a billion photographs of buildings with zero labels. Pretty useless. What are you going to do with that? You’d probably have to hit up Scale AI or Mercor to get people to label that data for you. Now imagine every single photo has a sticky note on the back that says: “Taken at 40.7128° N, 74.0060° W, facing northwest at a 15° upward angle, at 3:47 PM on a rainy Tuesday in October, by someone walking at 1.2 mph.” Much more useful! Suddenly, that photo isn’t just a picture. It’s a precisely located, precisely oriented window into a specific moment in physical space.

That’s what Niantic had. For 30 billion images. Across over 10 million scanned locations worldwide, with roughly 1 million new scans submitted every week.

And because millions of different players scanned the same locations at different times of day, in different weather, across different seasons, from different heights and angles, Niantic ended up with the kind of rich, varied dataset that’s essentially impossible to collect through traditional means. You can’t hire photographers to visit a million locations hundreds of times each. But with the opportunity of catching a Jigglypuff or a Squirtle or an ultra-rare Galarian Zapdos, Pokémon Go players did exactly that. I’m telling you, the power of Squirtle is not something to be trifled with.

Next, so how do you go from 30 billion tagged photos to a model that can tell a robot exactly where it is? There are three stages.

Stage 1: Building the 3D Map.

There’s a technique called Structure from Motion (SfM). If you have dozens of photos of the same object from different angles, you can mathematically figure out the 3D shape of that object. Just as your brain uses both eyes to calculate depth, SfM uses multiple photos from different positions to triangulate depth and geometry. Niantic ran this on every location with enough scan coverage. For each of its million-plus hotspot locations, they had a 3D mesh, a mathematical description of where every surface, edge, and corner sits in space.

Think of it like sculpting a 3D model of a building without the blueprint, but by analyzing how the building appeared in 500 different photographs.

Stage 2: Training the Neural Network.

Niantic then built what they call a Large Geospatial Model (LGM). They’re positioning this as the spatial equivalent of a Large Language Model. Same concept, different domain. Instead of learning patterns in text, it learns patterns in physical space.

Training works by showing the model an image and asking it to guess the location. You compare that guess to the known answer (the metadata you already have). If you’ve played GeoGuessr, this is basically that but on steroids. The gap between the guess and the answer is called the loss. You nudge the model’s internal settings slightly toward producing a better guess. Repeat this billions of times.

After enough repetitions, the model stops guessing randomly and starts recognizing patterns. It learns that this combination of building angles, shadow, and brick texture consistently appears at this specific location. It develops what you might call spatial intuition.

It’s like teaching a kid to recognize their own neighborhood. At first, they have no idea where they are. But after walking the same streets thousands of times, they start to notice: “When I see the blue house with the broken fence, I’m two blocks from home.” The model does this, but for a million locations simultaneously, and with mathematical precision.

Stage 3: Inference, The Model in the Real World.

Now enter a robot! Woohoo! We have our main character moment. In this example, it’s Coco, the delivery robot. Coco is out here on the sidewalk in Los Angeles. Coco doesn’t have a clear GPS signal because it’s surrounded by tall buildings and needs to know exactly where it is. Oh no, what to do. Coco’s camera takes a quick burst of images. Those images get sent to Niantic’s model. The model looks at what’s visible, the facade of a specific building, the curve of a particular corner, the texture of a specific wall, and matches it against everything it learned during training. Within seconds, it returns: “You are at this precise coordinate, facing this direction, at this height.” Phew! Coco now knows with the highest level of accuracy where it is and where it’s supposed to go. Coco is happy.

Think of it like this. You’re blindfolded and dropped somewhere in your city. Someone removes the blindfold for five seconds, then puts it back on. In those five seconds, you look around and immediately recognize: “I’m on the corner of 5th and Main, facing the library.” That’s what the model is doing, except it works in a million cities it’s never physically been to, because it’s seen them ten thousand times through players’ phones.

With all of this, Niantic built Lightship, a developer platform built on top of all this data, and created VPS (Visual Positioning System). Niantic opened VPS to third-party developers, allowing any AR app or robotics company to use it.

Why This Is So Fascinating & My Hypothesis

Now that we’ve gone through the full story, from Pokémon Go’s origins to Coco delivery robots using its data to deliver pizza, let me get to why I find this relationship between Pokémon Go and physical AI so fascinating. And why I think this matters way beyond just Niantic.

My hypothesis: the secret sauce to scalable data infrastructure in the physical AI world might lie in consumer apps.

What was magical and powerful about Pokémon Go was that it got people to VOLUNTARILY walk to places they would never have before. People didn’t even think of “data collection” when they were on the app; they were just voluntarily collecting data for fun.

Compare this to traditional data collection. Waymo has raised over $16 billion and spent years sending specialized cars through cities to collect autonomous driving data. Google Street View requires a fleet of vehicles driving every street on Earth. Niantic got millions of people to do something similar for free, because they were having fun.

And this isn’t the first time someone has done this. Many apps have been doing it in various ways:

Waze built real-time traffic AI by convincing people to report accidents they see in the community. That’s crowdsourcing. It was sold to Google for $1.1 billion.

reCAPTCHA, the blurry photos that Google asks you to click on to verify you’re not a robot? That’s basically you labeling training data for Google Maps.

Duolingo users think they’re learning Spanish. Yes, they are, but they’re also labeling linguistic data and training translation models.

The pattern is always the same: design the app so that normal usage generates the data you need. The data product and the consumer product are the same product. The user just doesn’t know it (or maybe they do), but they don’t consciously think about it.

The Data Bottleneck in Physical AI (and How Consumer Apps Can Help)

So let me zoom out and talk about why this matters so much right now. To do that, I want to talk about some basic robotics 101, just so we're on the same page.

To build robots that function in the real world, there are many, many parts. At the core, you can think of a couple of main components:

Sensors come first: how machines see.

  • There are a couple of ways to go about this. You can do this with Stereo Vision. Stereo Vision is basically using two cameras, like your two eyes, to compare the two images and figure out how the object changes from one image to another. That delta is how it knows depth, how far something is.

  • You can also use LiDAR, the use of thousands of laser pulses to measure how long it takes for the light to bounce back from the object. The time it takes for that light to get back represents the distance.

  • You can also use Monocular Depth Estimation: one camera and an algorithm. This is basically what our phones do. A single camera, but you slap a neural network on top of that, trained on millions of images to learn to estimate depth from visual cues. It’s less precise, but it is a lot cheaper than LiDAR, which is why it scales to consumer devices.

From the sensors, you build a perception model that basically maps what they are saying. This teaches the robot what it sees.

From there, you teach it a world model, which allows it to predict what will happen next.

Then you teach it a policy model, which allows it to decide what actions to take.

Lastly is the control system. TLDR: a way in which it can execute the things it sees and the decisions it makes.

The challenge running through all of this is the same: data. LLMs were built on text data: Reddit, books, Wikipedia, and the vast library of written language already available online. That’s what allows current LLMs to be trained. But when it comes to physical AI in the world, you start missing a bunch of data. You need spatial and body real-world data, and there’s just no internet equivalent of this. Every robotics company is facing the same thing: there’s just not enough real-world data out there.

There are a couple of approaches people use to bridge this gap.

  • First is Human Teleoperation, aka a human controls the robot manually while the system records everything. Think camera feeds, motor commands, sensor data, and then the robot learns to imitate the human. 1X does this with its NEO humanoid, in which remote “1X Experts” guide the robot through unfamiliar household chores, and each session doubles as training data. Sanctuary AI does something similar with their Phoenix humanoid, but added tactile sensors so operators actually feel what the robot touches, generating richer data. Telexistence in Japan launched an entire service around this in January; they generate large-scale robotic motion datasets on demand for customers.

  • The second approach is Simulation, where you build a physical simulation of the environment and generate millions of examples in a virtual environment. This is the fastest and cheapest method, and a lot of people are using it right now. NVIDIA is basically the backbone here with Isaac Sim, their open-source robotics simulation platform. Fanuc just partnered with NVIDIA to create photorealistic digital twins of entire factories, train robots virtually, and then deploy them. Tesla also leans heavily on simulation for Optimus, training in virtual environments before the real world.

  • The last one is training on YouTube videos of what humans do in a given task. The model learns what a correct position looks like by watching the videos. Rhoda AI just raised $450 million specifically to train robots using publicly available internet videos; that’s their whole thesis. Skild AI takes a similar approach, learning from large datasets of videos of human activity. Meta has been exploring this too, training robots on egocentric video from their Ego4D dataset, basically first-person footage of people doing everyday things like grocery shopping and cooking.

The best combination would be all three: pre-train on video and simulation, fine-tune on real-world demonstrations. But that last part, the real-world data, is the bottleneck. Simulation can only help so much. There’s still a huge gap between simulation and the real world.

What consumer apps like Pokémon Go do is crowdsource 3D mapping of the real world and, in the process, generate training data for robotics. This is huge because you’re building a dataset from different angles, with real people, with real-world evidence. What else is going to collect real-world data from a variety of angles, in cities everywhere, at this scale?

And the third ingredient, which people consistently underestimate, is data curation. Thirty billion images sounds impressive. The number that actually makes it into training is much smaller. You have to throw out blurry photos, locations with too few scans, conflicting data, and outliers. Bad training data doesn’t produce a bad model; it produces a confidently wrong model. Which is worse. This is why Scale AI, valued at over $29 billion, built its entire business on providing human labelers for AI training data.

The Starfield Library Moment

A recent experience I had personally really highlighted this for me. I was traveling around in Korea and visited the Starfield Library, a massive, architecturally stunning library in Seoul. It’s a library, so some people are supposed to be sitting and reading, but instead I saw like 5,000 different people with their phones out, taking photos and streaming videos of themselves walking up and down the staircase over and over and over again.

To be realistic, sure, they’ll upload some of the best photos to Instagram or TikTok. But the majority of those images and videos are just stuck on their phones. Doing nothing, eating up storage.

That’s interesting and a little sad, isn’t it? Because had those artifacts been part of a data-collection infrastructure, that would have been extremely valuable data right there.

What I’m seeing in the world is that our lives are built on phones. Our lives are captured in videos, photos, memos, and written notes everywhere, but a lot of that data is just stuck. It hasn’t been transformed into something useful. Not in a way that Reddit data is currently useful for a GPT model, for example.

At this point, we can easily move into a discussion of privacy, and by no means am I trivializing this topic. To use consumer-generated data ethically to train models is a whole other conversation that’s worthy of its own stage. There should, for sure, be guardrails on what data should or should not be collected and included in data training. By no measure is this an easy decision. I also personally believe one should know they are partaking in model training if they are, in fact, doing so. I strongly believe in the power of integrity and consent. The key to solving this problem, in my opinion, lies in the design of incentives. Companies should think critically about incentive alignment to get users to partake in data collection and model training. To build, we need data. There’s straight up no way around that. However, I stand strong on the assertion that we should always do so CONSENSUALLY.

The TLDR here is that if everyone’s already taking tons of photos and videos, might as well use them for something useful consensually, other than hogging phone storage.

Last couple of thoughts

The real moat Niantic built was a data flywheel. The more users they have, the more data they capture, the better model they can train, the more their product is enhanced, and thus the more users they have. And because the map is alive, new buildings go up, old ones come down, seasons change, and the data collection is ongoing. Players walking past a newly constructed building automatically update the living map.

I wonder if there’s a way to turn consumer-produced data into something better for training robotic models. My prediction is that in the next decade or so, we’ll see more and more of the data that physical AI needs collected not just in labs but in the real world, by regular people using consumer apps. The challenge then is: how do we ensure the data is high quality? How do we make sense of it? How do we turn it into something useful?

I think that’s going to be the next bottleneck. But I do see the potential for an infrastructure to collect the data we need, not just from one lab but from many people.
