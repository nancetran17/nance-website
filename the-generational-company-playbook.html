The Generational Company Playbook, Part I: Problems that Move Civilizations for Generations

The Civilization Flourishment Index: A Framework to Guide us Forward

Nancy TranAug 24, 2025Read on Substack

I don’t know when it started, but at some point, all I ever talk about is wanting to create generational companies. I either want to work at one, create one, or invest in one. That means I need clear definitions and qualifications. That’s what we’re doing today - a deep dive into the first component of what makes a generational company.

Before that, a quick intro: what comprises a generational company? Three things. A generational company does these for decades:

  1. They solve generational problems. This one is hard. (In this post, we’ll dive into what qualifies a problem as “generational.”)

  2. They execute exceptionally.

  3. They keep reinventing themselves (to keep doing #1 and #2 because problem changes shape and the bar for “exceptional execution” rises)

This post focuses on #1 - which I’d argue is the most important. If you pick the wrong problem, #2 and #3 genuinely don’t matter.

Why now? Why think about any of this?

Because it’s too easy to drift. Because “AI will do it” is not a plan. Because people still matter - deeply - and our job is to aim talent and capital at work that moves humanity forward, not sideways. And because writing this forces me to say what I believe and own the risk of being wrong in public (which can be humiliating - but it’s fine - we all need a little spice in life)

So - what is a generational problem?

Step 1: Definition of a Generational Problem

A generational problem has the following components:

  • Scale & universality. It can affect hundreds of millions (ideally billions) of people. It’s cross-demographic and not constrained to a niche; it persists across geographies, classes, and cultures. Classic domains that meet this bar include energy, healthcare, education, communication, and finance.

  • Multi-decade relevance. The problem has existed for decades and is likely to remain relevant for decades more. This is distinct from temporary inefficiencies and tied to structural forces. Examples include the need for clean water, the rising demand for healthspan, and productivity growth. Most real problems scale with one variable: people. We need tight definitions to test that a problem truly endures.

  • Structural transformation potential. Solving it rewires an industry or whole market; it doesn’t just help one or two teams. It makes people think differently about how to interact with the world or solve a category of problems, and it enables other innovations to exist or scale. Examples here include paradigm shifts like the internet, AI platforms, or renewable energy.

  • Moral & civilizational weight. It tackles issues at the core of human flourishing—safety, dignity, equity, creativity, purpose, and survival. Yes, I’m writing from a human perspective; that lens is the point (sorry, the doggos and cats in the world - we love you).

  • Asymmetric difficulty & opportunity. It requires breakthroughs in technology, distribution, or organizational models. Once solved, it unlocks compounding returns for decades. Things like quantum computing for materials science, nuclear fusion, AGI safety - they fit in this bucket.

If a problem clears four or five, it’s worth building your life around. If not, it might still be good work - just not civilization-changing.

Step 2: How to Qualify a Problem as “Generational”

Scale & Universality

Look for problems billions face or that underpin global-scale industries. These often seem basic or obvious: energy, communication, health, money, knowledge.

  • Diagnostic questions

    • How many people or businesses are affected today?

    • Is this problem relevant across geographies, classes, and demographics?

    • Does solving it improve the lives or productivity of hundreds of millions or more?

  • Answers you should be looking for

    • “Every business, from SMB to Fortune 500, struggles with this.”

    • “Billions of people will eventually need this solution, regardless of country.”

Endurance & Persistence

Solving enduring problems creates long-term stability and progress. Civilization moves forward only when solutions last longer than one technological or political cycle. To do this, we need to separate structural needs from temporary or trend demands.

  • Diagnostic Questions:

    • Has this problem existed for decades already?

    • If solved once, does it stay solved, or do new versions emerge?

    • Will it still be urgent in 20–50 years?

  • What are the answers you should be looking for?

    • “Humans will always need better ways to generate, store, and use energy.”

    • “Access to healthcare has been a problem for centuries and will remain so.”

Structural Transformation Potential

Societies leap forward when bottlenecks in systems are broken (electricity, the internet, vaccines). Transformational problems create new layers of civilization (e.g., the internet enabled e-commerce, social media, and telemedicine). To do this, we need to look for problems that have multiplier effects and power shifts.

  • Diagnostic Questions:

    • If solved, does this redefine how industries operate? Does solving this problem create new markets or unlock industries?

    • Does it enable new business models or entirely new categories of innovation?

    • Would the economy look fundamentally different afterward?

    • Does solving this problem rewire workflows, economies, or power structures?

  • What are the answers you should be looking for?

    • “The internet didn’t just improve communication - it created whole new sectors.”

    • “AI platforms will reorganize knowledge work itself.”

Moral & Civilizational Weight

These are do-or-die problems: energy, health, truth, survival. Without progress, humanity risks decline, inequality, or collapse. Solving them creates dignity, opportunity, and safety at scale. These problems usually connect to civilizational needs (climate, healthcare, food, security, truth) and solving them increases human potential or reduces existential risks.

  • Diagnostic Questions:

    • If we don’t solve this, what happens to society in 50 years?

    • Does this problem touch on survival, equity, or dignity at a civilizational level?

    • Does it expand human potential, creativity, or safety?

  • What are the answers you should be looking for?

    • “Without addressing climate change, parts of Earth become uninhabitable.”

    • “If we don’t solve information trust, democracy collapses.”

Asymmetric Difficulty & Opportunity

Humanity advances when we solve hard problems that unlock decades of compounding benefits (electricity grids, spaceflight, AI). Difficult problems create step-change improvements, not just incremental ones. To identify these, look for technical or organizational breakthroughs that are required. True generational problems often have a “cold start” difficulty that deters most entrants.

  • Diagnostic Questions:

    • Why hasn’t this been solved yet?

    • Does solving this require new technology, business models, or distribution channels?

    • Once solved, does it unlock enduring advantages (network effects, moats, scale)?

  • What are the answers you should be looking for?

    • “It requires fundamental breakthroughs in energy storage, but once achieved, it changes everything.”

    • “The upfront investment is massive, but the compounding effects are enormous.”

Step 3: How to solve a Generational Problem? What does good solving even look like?

We toss around “forward” a lot - move civilization forward, move intelligence forward, move equity forward. In a vacuum, “forward” is meaningless. Forward to where? On what scale? Toward what target?

In my opinion, “forward” is progress in the conditions for human flourishing and survival. It’s not just GCP growth or tech advancement - it’s about creating a world where:

  • Humans can survive and thrive safely for centuries

  • We expand our capabilities - individually, collectively, and technologically

  • We increase dignity, equity, and agency, so more people can participate in that thriving

  • We preserve optionality for future generations, rather than closing off possibilities. 

Simple Equation: Forward = More Survival + More Thriving + More Freedom to Flourish

To keep this grounded, here are established frameworks used to measure how a civilization is doing:

Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs (Maslow)

Maslow’s model (1943, later refined) stacks human needs from physiological and safety through belonging and esteem to self-actualization, sometimes ending with self-transcendence. At a population level, it’s proxied chiefly by food security, housing stability, public safety, social connection, educational attainment, mental health, creative participation, and time for leisure - rather than directly measured. 

Physiological → Safety → Love/Belonging → Esteem → Self-Actualization → Self-transcendence

The core idea is simple: a flourishing civilization is one in which more people spend more of their lives on the upper rungs (creativity, meaning) instead of fighting for survival, and the target is that over time, more people will move up this ladder of needs.

Kardashev Scale (Energy-Based Civilization Scale, Kardashev)

Kardashev (1964) proposed ranking civilizations by usable energy: Type I harnesses all planetary energy, Type II captures a star’s output (think Dyson-sphere concepts), and Type III taps a galaxy. 

Type I = All planetary energy (Earth ~0.73 today).
Type II = All energy from a star (e.g., Dyson sphere).
Type III = All energy from a galaxy.

We estimate position via total power consumption and capture; Earth is still below Type I, with practical proxies like per-capita energy use, clean-energy share, storage, and transmission capacity. Energy is the capability budget for everything else - science, computation, comfort—though raw watts say nothing about justice or happiness.

Human Development Index (HDI, United Nations)

HDI combines life expectancy, education (mean and expected years of schooling), and income per capita (PPP, log-scaled) into a 0–1 score using a geometric mean. Compiled annually from standardized national data, it moves beyond GDP to track the basics of human development and the capacity to choose a life one values. The primary civilizational application of this framework is to monitor the extent to which humans are living with dignity and health.

OECD Better Life Index (BLI)

The BLI is a multidimensional well-being dashboard across eleven domains - housing, income, jobs, community, education, environment, governance, health, life satisfaction, safety, and work-life balance - mixing objective statistics with subjective surveys. It paints how people actually live, not just what they earn, though coverage skews toward OECD members, and cultural differences can color self-reports. This framework paints a broader picture of well-being - how people actually live, not just what they earn. On a civilization scale, it gives us an idea of what flourishing looks like wholistically.

Planetary Boundaries Framework (Stockholm Resilience Center)

Developed by the Stockholm Resilience Centre, Planetary Boundaries defines nine Earth-system guardrails—climate change, biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater change, biogeochemical flows (nitrogen and phosphorus), ocean acidification, stratospheric ozone, atmospheric aerosols, and novel entities like chemical pollution - each with control variables ( (e.g., CO₂ ppm, biodiversity loss rates) with a “safe operating space.”

Overshooting these raises the risk of systemic ecological collapse; staying within them protects the physical base of civilization. Boundaries have uncertainty, and global measures can mask local variation, but as a compass, they are vital.

The Great Filter & Existential Risk (Nick Bostrom, etc.)

The Great Filter is the idea, popularized in futures and philosophy (e.g., Bostrom), that somewhere on the path from simple life to galaxy-spanning civilization lies a hard barrier; in policy terms it becomes a portfolio of existential risks - nuclear war, engineered pandemics, unaligned AI, runaway climate, plus low-probability natural shocks - that could permanently curtail humanity’s potential. This framework matters because everything else is useless if we can’t survive and keep the future open. A civilization’s success is measured by whether, collectively as a species, we can avoid and survive existential risks and continue to grow.

Gross National Happiness (GNH, Bhutan)

GNH is both a philosophy and an index that centers well-being over output across nine domains: psychological well-being, health, education, time use, cultural diversity and resilience, good governance, community vitality, ecological diversity and resilience, and living standards.

It blends surveys with objective indicators to classify sufficiency across domains. This framework matters because it reweights the scoreboard toward meaning, connection, and culture, not just production. Flourishment isn’t just economic, and this framework takes into account psychological well-being, community vitality, culture, health, and environment. On a civilizational scale, this framework places human happiness at the center.

By combining all these frameworks, you create a somewhat interesting amalgamation of all the characteristics of good frameworks, and you make what I call the Civilization Flourishment Index (CFI), composed of 5 components. And ideally, the goal of progress is to move all five axes upward simultaneously in ways that are durable, scalable, and transformational.

Civilization Flourishment Index (CFI)

Axis 1: Survival & Resilience

Survival & Resilience is about a civilization’s capacity to avoid existential risks and absorb shocks without losing its future. In practice, that spans climate stability (emissions trajectories, temperature anomalies, carbon concentrations), nuclear posture and de-escalation regimes, biosecurity surveillance and response times, AI safety and alignment practices, ecological footprint and regeneration, as well as infrastructure redundancy, civil defense, and even multi-planet optionality. 

Definition: Humanity’s ability to avoid existential risks and build long-term resilience
Measures: Climate stability, nuclear safety, biosecurity, AI alignment, planetary sustainability, multi-planet presence.
Scale:
1 = Civilization constantly at risk of collapse (climate tipping points, pandemics)
10 = Humanity is multi-planetary, existential risks near zero

A civilization near 1/10 lives close to tipping points - pandemics, conflict, supply-chain fragility - while a 10/10 world is multi-planetary, weathering disruptions gracefully, with aligned machine systems and a robust ecological base.

Axis 2: Health & Longevity

Health & Longevity captures whether people live long, healthy lives that they can actually enjoy. The signals are familiar but nontrivial: life expectancy and healthspan, preventable mortality, disease burden (DALYs), mental-health prevalence and treatment access, immunization coverage, primary-care reach, and affordability. 

Definition: Humans living long, healthy, flourishing lives
Measures: Life expectancy, healthspan, disease burden, mental health.
Scale:
1 = Life expectancy ~30 years, high infant mortality
10 = Indefinite healthy lifespan, diseases largely eliminated

A 1/10 landscape looks like a life expectancy around thirty with high infant mortality and endemic disease; a 10/10 future looks like near-indefinite healthy years for most people, with dramatic reductions in chronic disease and universal access to timely, effective care.

Axis 3: Knowledge & Capability

Knowledge & Capability reflects what humanity can understand, build, and control. It blends energy capture and reliability (including clean share and storage), scientific output and translation (R&D intensity, researchers per capita, citations, patents), computational capacity, education access and attainment, broadband reach, and off-planet reach (launch cadence, deep-space missions). 

Definition: Humanity’s capacity to understand, create, and control its environment.
Measures: Energy capture, education access, scientific output, computational/AI capability, space exploration.
Scale:
1 = Illiteracy, no science, subsistence living.
10 = Type II Kardashev (stellar energy), near-complete mastery of physics/biology.

At 1/10, illiteracy and subsistence dominate; at 10/10, you’re approaching a Type II Kardashev civilization with abundant clean energy, ubiquitous education, massive open compute, and near-complete command of the physical and biological sciences.

Axis 4: Equity and Dignity

Equity & Dignity asks whether opportunity, rights, and the upside of progress are broadly shared - and whether people are treated with respect along the way. You see it in poverty rates and income/wealth distribution, social mobility, equal access to education, healthcare, finance, and legal identity, the rule of law and human-rights protections, freedom from discrimination, and the affordability of connection to the digital economy. 

Definition: The fairness of access to opportunity, rights, and flourishing.
Measures: Poverty rates, inequality (Gini coefficient), access to education/finance/health, human rights.
Scale:
1 = Extreme inequality, majority in poverty/slavery.
10 = Universal access to dignity, opportunity, and self-determination.

A 1/10 world tolerates slavery, caste, or extreme exclusion; a 10/10 world delivers universal protections and high mobility, with fair access to the ingredients of a good life.

Axis 5: Creativity & Meaning

Creativity & Meaning measures whether people have the time, tools, and freedom to do more than survive: to make, explore, contemplate, and belong. Useful proxies include time-use and leisure, participation in arts and cultural production, support for curiosity-driven research, availability of public spaces and libraries, community and civic engagement, freedom of expression, and subjective well-being. 

Definition: The extent to which humans can transcend survival/productivity and express creativity, art, exploration, and meaning.
Measures: Leisure time, cultural output, research not tied to survival, space for art/philosophy/spirituality.
Scale:
1 = All human effort goes to survival.
10 = Widespread flourishing of creativity and meaning, survival needs fully handled.

A 1/10 society spends nearly all human effort on bare survival; a 10/10 society sees widespread creative expression, exploration, and interior life - because survival needs are handled and dignity is real.

And with that, we have our very first component of a generational company.

So when you ask “is this a generational problem?”, the meta-question becomes:
Does solving this problem move humanity forward on the survival–thriving–freedom scale, and does it do so at a civilizational scale?

Humanity Today (2025, Approximate CFI)

If you compress the dashboard to a snapshot, today looks roughly like this:

  • Survival & Resilience: 3/10 (climate crisis, AI risk, fragile geopolitics).

  • Health & Longevity: 6/10 (life expectancy ~73 years, but chronic disease dominates).

  • Knowledge & Capability: 7/10 (AI breakthroughs, internet, space exploration).

  • Equity & Dignity: 4/10 (extreme inequality, billions without basic access).

  • Creativity & Meaning: 5/10 (more freedom than ever, but unevenly distributed).

Composite CFI ≈ 5.0/10 → Humanity is midway: past primitive survival, but far from flourishing sustainably.

Survival & Resilience: 3/10

We are not doing too well here, friends. 2024 was confirmed as the warmest year on record and the first full calendar year averaging above 1.5°C over pre-industrial levels, while scientists now judge six of nine planetary boundaries (e.g., climate, biosphere integrity) to be transgressed - clear signs our life-support systems are under stress. Meanwhile, the global nuclear stockpile is growing again (≈12,100 warheads in 2024; more deployed and more states modernizing), and the world is only beginning to coordinate on AI risk (Bletchley Declaration; first UN GA consensus AI resolution in 2024). Those moves are positives - but they’re early governance signals, not risk elimination. We definitely need to do better on this axis!

Health & Longevity: 6/10

The world has roughly doubled life expectancy in a century; ~73 years globally by 2023 is a civilizational win. But the quality of those added years is uneven: non-communicable diseases (cardio, cancer, diabetes) now account for ~75% of non-pandemic deaths; antimicrobial resistance directly killed ~1.27M people in 2019 and was associated with ~4.95M deaths; and mental disorders affect ~1 in 8 people worldwide, straining underfunded systems. Care delivery remains heavily paperwork-intensive and of uneven quality; prevention is underfunded; aging populations strain the healthcare system. We’re past the halfway mark because the baseline is so much better than it used to be - but not close to healthy longevity for all.

Knowledge & Capability: 7/10

Our capacity to understand and shape the environment is high and rising. Good job, Earth! We did something meaningful so far. Renewables hit a record 30% of global electricity in 2023 (and continued to climb in 2024), over 5.5B people (~68%) are online, and the Stanford AI Index 2025 documents rapid AI capability gains and diffusion. We’ve built a planet-scale information network, mass literacy, modern science, cheap sensors, industrial robotics, and frontier AI. Launch cadence is up; private space is real; compute is abundant (if not evenly distributed).

Yet we’re still far from Type-I energy mastery; clean, firm power isn’t universal; education quality and research translation are wildly uneven; compute and broadband access are not a given for billions.

Equity & Dignity: 4/10

Extreme poverty has fallen dramatically over the decades, but billions still lack at least one basic secure income, safe housing, consistent healthcare, quality education, legal identity, or digital access. Oooff… As of 2022, 2.2 billion people lacked safely managed drinking water; ~251 million children and youth remain out of school; extreme poverty progress has stalled or reversed in many fragile states; and ~2.6 billion people remain offline.

Wealth and opportunity are concentrated; social mobility is uneven; discrimination and rights violations persist; authoritarian drift and conflict displace tens of millions. Even where services exist, they’re often unaffordable or of low quality.

Creativity & Meaning: 5/10

The tools to make, share, explore, and learn have never been more accessible (vibe-coders - yes, you are benefiting from this); cultural output is exploding; more people than ever can spend time above subsistence. But time poverty, precarious work, loneliness, and attention-harvesting platforms erode depth. Public spaces, arts, and curiosity-driven research are underfunded in many places; speech and cultural freedom are constrained in others. We’re squarely in the middle: unprecedented opportunity to create, unevenly realized. I’m telling you - The Great Gatsby was on to something.

Composite ≈ 5/10

Add it up, and the median human experience sits around “no longer primitive, not yet flourishing.” We’ve made meaningful improvements in capability and health; however, survival risk, inequality, and shallow meaning continue to hold us back.

TLDR, we are no longer cavemen - good job, guys! Now, keep going.

Why does any of this matter to me?

Before I end the post, I would like to go back to this question. Why on earth does any of this matter? Why should one be going around looking for generational problems to solve?

I keep circling the same belief: life is about suffering, and life's purpose is to overcome it.

By now, you can tell I’m high-key writing this for myself. If you’re bored, go scroll TikTok. If you’re still here, my reasons are simple: in going through suffering and coming out of it, I want to make the world a little better for the next generation. “Better” means maximum experiences, maximum emotions, maximum memories, maximum imagination, and maximum love.

If I can do that, even a little, the next generation inherits a world with more options, more dignity, more room to experience life fully.

I want fewer people to feel irrelevant in the process.
I want my work - my words - to outlast me.
I want to dedicate my existence to problems worthy of a lifetime.
