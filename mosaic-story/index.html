The Mosaic Story

I would argue that surgery is the closest modern medicine has come to defeating death.

Three outcomes

For most of history, if something mechanically catastrophic happened inside the body from a ruptured appendix, to an obstructed childbirth, an ectopic pregnancy, an intestinal obstruction, a congenital heart defect, or a major internal bleeding there was often very little medicine could do. The patient either recovered spontaneously or died.

Modern surgery created a third possibility: physically change the anatomy and physically remove the cause of death.

01–03 150 years

So modern surgery has spent the last 150 years becoming less invasive, to reduce the physical cost of accessing anatomy. We went from opening the body, to small incisions, to navigating through the body’s own vascular highways.

01 Open surgery
  • In open surgery, the surgeon can see the organ. They can touch it. They can feel how the tissue responds.
02 Laparoscopy
  • In laparoscopy, we give up touch, but we preserve vision through a camera.
03 Endovascular
  • And then there is endovascular surgery, where a physician can make a needle puncture in the wrist or groin and navigate a surgical instrument deep inside the body, to the heart, the brain, the kidneys, or the legs.
A transport network

In other words, over the last few decades, physicians discovered that the vascular system can be used as a transportation network through the human body, and the implications have been enormous. Operations that once required large incisions can increasingly be performed through a needle-sized access point, to reach almost every major organ. Recovery gets shorter. Physiological trauma decreases. Patients leave the hospital sooner. Costs go down. And entirely new categories of targeted treatment become possible.

Heart, brain, limbs

It is one of the greatest substitution stories in medicine. So far, we used these pathways primarily to treat cardiovascular disease, to open blocked coronary arteries, stop strokes, repair aneurysms, or restore blood flow to a limb.

Every major organ

But increasingly, physicians are using the vascular system as a route to disease itself. They navigate to the liver to treat tumors. To the uterus to treat fibroids. To the prostate. To the lungs. And instead of delivering only balloons and stents, they can deliver drugs, radiation, energy, embolic agents, and other therapies precisely where they are needed. It is difficult to overstate what this means. We have discovered a way to reach deep inside the human body without opening it.

The expensive tradeoff

Endovascular medicine is an extraordinary access system, but it came with an expensive tradeoff: as the incision disappeared, direct human perception disappeared with it. Consider what the operator actually has to do: the physician’s hands are manipulating an instrument whose tip is more than a meter away. Inside an opaque, deformable, millimeter-scale blood vessel. With no direct line of sight.

04 Fluoroscopy

And this is what they see.

05 A tunnel without lights

Imagine driving through a tunnel without lights. In a car without windows. With only intermittent views from grainy satellite images. And from that, combined with subtle changes in resistance at your fingertips and years of accumulated experience, you infer what is happening.

06 One human at a time

What makes that possible today is extreme human expertise. We have created extraordinary medicine that is extraordinarily difficult to scale.

As a result, surgery may be the last $100B industry that still scales one highly trained human at a time. If you want to perform twice as many operations, you need at least twice as many surgeons, residents, and twice as many scrub nurses, circulating nurses, and radiology techs.

07 Output vs people

Manufacturing found ways to separate output from individual craftsmanship. Software created products that can serve millions of people with very little incremental human labor. Transportation is beginning to automate a task that, for more than a century, required one driver for every moving vehicle. But surgery is still bound by a linear labor-to-output relationship.

So we started asking a very fundamental question.

What would it take to scale the knowledge of the very best surgeons?

A navigation problem

Endovascular surgery is a navigation problem, and every navigation problem begins with two questions:

  • Where am I? And what's around me?
08 Seeing in the dark

Before you can choose where to go, you need an internal representation of the world. Humans tend to equate perception with optical vision. But nature doesn't. If you're a bat operating in darkness, vision isn't particularly useful. You use ultrasound. Other animals detect electrical fields, vibration, pressure, polarization, temperature. Evolution selects the sensory system that is useful for your environment.

09 An autonomous vehicle

Engineering works the same way. An autonomous vehicle needs depth perception, visible spectrum so it combines cameras, radar, LiDAR, inertial sensing, localization and maps.

Each sensor sees a different aspect of reality. The system fuses them together to answer exactly the same questions: Where am I? What's around me?

09 The lesson from AVs

And this brings us to one of the most important lessons from the autonomous vehicle industry.

For a long time, autonomous driving looked primarily like a decision-making problem. How should the car behave? When should it brake? When should it change lanes? How do you program driving? But none of that matters if the vehicle doesn't have a sufficiently accurate model of the world around it. Before the car could plan, it had to perceive. Perception creates a machine-readable representation of the environment. Only then could prediction become reliable. Only then could planning become useful. Only then could control become increasingly autonomous.

But perception had to come first.

10 At Mosaic

We think surgery needs to follow the same path. At Mosaic, we build surgical instruments that can perceive and navigate human anatomy. We are starting in the vascular system because we believe that solving vascular navigation would unlock access to delivering any therapy to any organ, anywhere.

10 Step one

Much like AVs did, we start by

  • Restorning perception:
    • Our technology keeps the human at the wheel, but it embeds sensing directly at the surgical tool–anatomy interface and reconstructs the vascular environment from the inside as the physician moves through it.
    • This gives the physician the immediate benefit of better understanding of the anatomical scene which in turn they use to navigate more safely.
11 Step two
  • Generate understanding
    • Once the environment itself can be perceived by a machine, those interactions become data.
    • This means the system can begin to understand what happened, what went wrong and how the expert operator recovered. Across enough cases, it can learn which states precede success, what patterns precede difficulty and what should happen next.
11 Step three
  • Closing the loop on Action
    • Better perception coupled with understanding, directly enables more precise control. Our second generation of products adds dexterity and fine motion control a meter away from the physician’s hands, leveraging on board perception

Surgical intelligence emerges from the bottom up.

  • Perception creates data.
  • Data creates understanding.
  • Understanding enables increasingly intelligent action.

That is how every major embodied intelligent system has developed. And we believe surgery will be no different.

For the last 150 years, we made surgery progressively less invasive. The next era is about making it progressively more intelligent.

Because once surgery can be perceived, it can be learnt. Once it can be learnt, it can be scaled.
