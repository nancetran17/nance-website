1 · Opening Notes

The history of surgery has been defined by slow but transformative shifts. From the advent of anesthesia in the 19th century, to aseptic technique, the rise of imaging, the arrival of minimally invasive tools and finally robotics. Each leap followed a single consistent thread: increased safety, precision, and reach.

But the findings are all too familiar. New tools for mitral valve repair. More compliant grafts. 3D printing for “patient-specific” devices. Innovations feel bounded, marginal, and destined for the slow cycle of medtech purgatory.

1 · Opening Notes

The realization comes only years later. That the true frontier is not a single procedure, device, or disease. It is the practice of surgery itself, and the way it is learned. The way knowledge is passed - or more often - trapped.

1 · Opening Notes

Over two millennia ago, Aristotle described two kinds of knowledge. Episteme, an explicit, codifiable knowledge that can be written in a manual or encoded in an algorithm. And techne, a knowledge that’s implicit, incommunicable except through the practice of craft, absorbed through apprenticeship and embodied in muscle, reflex, and intuition.

1 · Opening Notes

While basic surgical proficiency is epistemic, mastery is almost entirely techne. It resides in the hands and minds of individual practitioners. It is shaped by tacit sensory cues, refined over decades of repetition, and impossible to fully verbalize. This is the intuition that lets an expert anticipate how tissue will behave or sense danger before it happens.

It is both the treasure trove of surgical knowledge, as well as its most dangerous bottleneck.

Section 2

The Prospect of Surgical Autonomy

At the most fundamental level, we believe that healthcare access is the foundation of human progress. Surgery is the industry’s economic engine, driving 60% of hospital revenue, but also its most inflationary cost center. With cost pressures from demographic trends and compounding labor shortages, it’s increasingly clear that we need to do a lot more, with less.

2 · The Prospect of Surgical Autonomy

Today, surgical outcomes still depend on who’s in the room. The result is a wide bell curve of outcomes, with high variability and strong operator dependency. In fact the most predictive factor in surgery is still the volume–outcome relationship. Today, mastery is forged through an artisanal apprenticeship and years of relentless repetition, bounded by the limits of human memory, dexterity, and endurance.

2 · The Prospect of Surgical Autonomy

This model will not scale.

Productivity has stagnated — throughput per surgeon has flatlined since the 1980s, and costs have more than doubled after adjusting for inflation. The result is a human-labor bottleneck with outcomes that hinge directly on which surgeon is on call, in which hospital, and after how many hours of lost sleep. Surgical performance is ultimately capped by the physical and cognitive limits of humans.

2 · The Prospect of Surgical Autonomy

Surgical autonomy changes the equation. Picture the surgical outcome distribution today: a wide bell curve, a function of high variability and operator dependency.

  • Autonomy compresses the variance (i.e. improves consistency);
  • Shifts the curve to the right (i.e. scales the best results);
  • Expands the total number of procedures that can be safely performed (i.e. increases access and capacity).
2 · The Prospect of Surgical Autonomy

If evolved past human constraints, surgical systems can move far beyond what any one surgeon achieves in a lifetime. Autonomy harnesses the scale, efficiency, and standardization that transformed industries from manufacturing to transportation, and brings it into the OR. It is a rare opportunity to replace variability with reliability, raise both the floor and ceiling of performance, and make world-class surgery available to every patient, everywhere. But are we even on the right path?

2 · The Prospect of Surgical Autonomy

Right now, we face structural limitations that will continue to keep a truly scalable, autonomous surgical reality out of reach:

  • Implicit Knowledge Lock-in – More than 90% of surgical expertise is implicit. Apprenticeship can only transmit the explicit portion; but the proprioceptive, and perceptual elements remain locked in the expert’s nervous system, retiring right along with them.
  • Data Scarcity – No large-scale, high-fidelity dataset captures operative surgical scenarios with the necessary granularity and sufficiency to digitize this knowledge.
  • Mindless Robots – Current robots see better, move steadier, and hold tools longer, but they do not learn. They remain expensive extensions of the human hand. They are unthinking machines
  • Outdated Regulation - Regulations are mostly tuned for static tools, not dynamic, learning systems. This is changing, with PCCPs and SaMD pathways beginning to acknowledge adaptive autonomy.
Section 3

The Autonomous Vehicles Industry: A Playbook for Autonomy

After decades of false starts, autonomous driving has recently crossed a threshold of safety, utility and value. The last 18 months felt like a breakthrough. AVs weren’t lacking decision-making capabilities or algorithms, but rather, they could not see and understand the world well enough for those decisions to be safe. The breakthrough was advanced perception, which was enabled by:

  • Hardware, through a new generation of sensors capturing richer, more diverse signals.
  • Algorithms, through transformer-based perception models moving beyond CNNs, and bringing global, long-term context to vision, by fusing multi-sensor data into coherent 3D world models. This is what enables the shift from interpretation to in-context prediction.
  • Data, through decades of real-world driving experience capturing the diversity of weather, lighting, and human behavior, enabling systems to robustly anticipate, not just detect.
3 · A Playbook for Autonomy

Once perception reached human-level reliability, the rest of the autonomy stack could finally shine, as planners had world models they could finally trust.

The events of the past 18 months prove that when the right sequence is followed, autonomy is achievable. And that sequence follows a predictable arc.

  • It begins with perception, through machines capable of sensing their environment (i.e. advanced perception modules like LiDAR, multi-camera vision, and ultrasonics)
  • It advances through intelligence, as the vehicles generate and collect progressively richer data and sensory inputs that enable prediction of outcomes, and planning of actions.
  • Finally comes autonomy, initially through narrow, supervised deployments that gradually expand into unsupervised control.
3 · A Playbook for Autonomy

This ladder is formalized in the Levels 0–5 framework for autonomous vehicles. Today’s surgical systems sit mostly at Level 1 or 2: robot assistance. Level 3, supervised autonomy, is rare. Levels 4 and 5 don’t exist, and possibly never will unless we build the right perception systems first, just as was done in cars, drones, and aircrafts.

Section 4

The Umwelt

As long as surgical knowledge remains implicit and undigitized, surgical progress will be capped by human limits and the promise of autonomy will stay out of reach. Autonomy and intelligence begin with perception, the foundation on which all natural embodied intelligence is built. If perception is the necessary prerequisite for autonomy, it’s imperative to ask: what kind of perception?

4 · The Umwelt

In robotics, the term “world model” is used often: a computational construct that represents an objective reality and predicts how that reality will change during physical, multi-agent interactions. But biology tells us the truth is more subjective. Every organism lives within an umwelt, its unique perceptual world defined by the sensory channels and cognitive processes it possesses. This umwelt is not a passive snapshot; it is actively shaped by the organism’s physical interactions with the world, evolving in a functional loop to serve survival, purpose, and engagement with the world around it.

4 · The Umwelt

In surgery, our tools do not have umwelt. No internal model of the world, no evolving loop between perception and action. If we wish to endow them with perception, we should not assume that an anthropocentric approach is optimal. Humans evolved as generalists, with senses tuned for survival in environments far removed from the operating field.

4 · The Umwelt

Why constrain machines to the limits of human perception? Surgical tools require a different perceptual architecture: high-resolution, multimodal sensing embedded at the tool–anatomy interface, optimized for the physical and functional realities of surgery. This is how a system develops its own umwelt - capturing, interpreting, and acting on information with a fidelity no human sensory stack can match.

4 · The Umwelt

The umwelt is therefore the foundation of embodied intelligence in surgery. Only when a system perceives through its own native senses can it model its environment, adapt in real time, and make implicit knowledge explicit, transforming the proprioceptive and anticipatory intuition that defines surgical mastery into something visible, recordable, and analyzable. A tool with its own umwelt can sense and encode these patterns, making them learnable, improvable, and replicable at scale, for the first time.

Section 5

Eras of Robotics in Surgery

The term robotics in medicine can be both inadequate and misleading. In common usage, it collapses several distinct paradigms under a single name. To understand where we are headed, it is worth disentangling them.

Section 5.1

Surgical Augmentation Platforms (SAP)

This is the era defined by systems such as the da Vinci robot, of Intuitive Surgical. Here, technology amplifies human capability: vision is magnified, hand tremor suppressed, tool motion scaled for precision. The defining feature is direct and continuous human control. The robot augments, but does not think.

Section 5.2

Surgical Cognition Assistants (SCA)

Intelligent surgical tools belong to a different class. By digitally capturing the umwelt of a tool (through its unique, purpose-specific sensory world of conductance, flow, vibration, and contact) the system externalizes surgical perception into a form that is recordable, transportable, and cumulative. It becomes a clinical memory: storing procedural experience, recognizing context, providing intraoperative reminders, and mentoring operators through prior cases and guidelines.

In this paradigm, autonomy initially remains fully vested in the surgeon. The system creates a shared layer of cognition between surgeon and tool, which is the foundation for intelligence that grows richer with every case and eventually leads to autonomy.

Section 5.3

Autonomous Surgical Systems (ASS)

Fully autonomous surgical systems would leverage perception, reasoning, and actuation to execute procedural steps with minimal human oversight. The promise is consistent optimal performance, scalability, and expanded access. But autonomy in surgery is not simply an engineering challenge; it is a clinical, ethical, and social one. The body is not a factory floor, and live tissue is not a predictable substrate.

5 · Eras of Robotics in Surgery

This is why autonomy in surgery must be approached as an evolution, not a leap. First augmentation, then cognition, and only then autonomy. Surgical Cognition Assistants (SCAs) provide the missing bridge: digitizing experience, building shared intelligence, and creating the vast procedural datasets from which autonomy can safely emerge. In this framing, autonomy is not an endpoint but a continuum. It is the asymptote of a learning system whose foundation is built case by case, surgeon by surgeon, until the accumulated intelligence itself becomes the operator.

Section 6

The perception-limited frontier: Vascular Surgery

We have insofar established that autonomous systems emerge from machines with perceptual awareness, and the ability to process and interpret the world around them to a degree of accuracy that enables safe, continuous interactions. To unlock surgical autonomy, we must:

  • Build reliable perception systems
  • Collect, store and learn upon vast amounts of data captured by those systems
6 · Vascular Surgery

Unlike Waymo, we cannot pay contractors to drive around San Francisco for years and collect a massive corpus of road data. Our business model must be built around orchestrated, high-value data accumulation. That means entering a domain where perception (independently from the future promise of autonomy) delivers immediate clinical value.

So where does perception make a transformational difference in surgery?

6 · Vascular Surgery

Incisions have gotten smaller and smaller, and the next logical frontier is incisionless surgery. In endovascular and percutaneous procedures, surgeons navigate the body through its natural highways - the blood vessels. This specialty has transformed what were once sternum-cracking, bloody operations into virtually incisionless interventions, making it one of the fastest growing specialties in the country. These procedures are also more cost-effective and have demosntrated better outcomes, but they are exponentially more complex for humans to do.

6 · Vascular Surgery

The challenges of this field create an opportunity for us:

  • Conventional cameras offer no utility in blood-filled vessels.
  • Surgeons operate at a distance, with no line-of-sight visibility
  • Perception is reduced to fragments, as subtle changes in resistance, torque, and low-resolution imaging cues
6 · Vascular Surgery

Guidewires and catheters are the standard tool for anatomical navigation. They live in a rich sensory world, with conductance, flow, vibration, contact, and dynamic geometry. Today, that information is filtered entirely through the surgeon’s hands and nervous system. The proprioceptive and anticipatory knowledge that guides the case is never recorded, never digitized, and never teachable beyond the apprenticeship model. We can change that by embedding umwelt at the tool-anatomy interface, augmenting the surgeon’s perception, and creating a data flywheel to power the future of autonomy.

Section 7

Mosaic Roadmap

Mosaic is a human-first autonomy company. We believe the best kind of intelligence is still human intelligence, and Mosaic is on the most human-aligned path to surgical autonomy, starting by digitizing perception at the tool-anatomy interface.

7 · Mosaic Roadmap

Mosaic extends the awareness of surgeons by building intelligent surgical tools that allow doctors to operate beyond their own cognitive and perceptual constraints, and unconstrained by today’s external imaging infrastructure. By turning standard vascular tools into live systems, we create a digital perception of the surgical environment in real time. Initially, the goal isn’t to replace the surgeon, but to augment their capabilities, reduce uncertainty, reduce reliance on radiation, and make complex procedures safer and more accessible.

7 · Mosaic Roadmap

Autonomy in medicine will follow a path similar to other industries: start with perception, then build intelligence, and only gradually introduce autonomy. We see surgical autonomy not as an endpoint, but as a continuum; one that transforms the act of surgery into a collaborative, adaptive, intelligent ensemble of surgeon-and-tool. The right way forward is not a sudden leap to “robot surgeons,” but a stepwise process where technology becomes a trusted partner: capturing experience, guiding decisions, and ultimately enabling care that is safer, more consistent, ultimately enabling a safer, more consistent standard of care worldwide.

Section 7.1

Act 1 - Perception: The Trojan Horse to surgical intelligence

The path to autonomy is always data-hungry and it starts with the digitization of human knowledge. For companies building in the physical world, this data is scarce and the act of collecting it is slow and expensive. This means it’s imperative to create an indepedent business case for data collection and progressively earn the right to collect high-quality data as the business operates. At Mosaic, we must do that by providing independent value to surgeons before we reach autonomy.

7.1 · Act 1 - Perception

By embedding sensors and edge computation into workhorse surgical tools like catheters and guidewires, we turn each procedure into a high-resolution data stream, that obviates the need for room-sized, radiation-based external imaging systems by offering intuitive “internal” imaging interfaces. Surgeons are among the most skilled human experts, and their procedural intuition cannot be captured through interviews or video. It must be digitized as they operate.

Section 7.2

Act 2 - Intelligence: The foundation of autonomy

Autonomy in surgery is a data problem, and today almost none of it is captured. Mosaic generates synchronized multi-modal data aligned with low-level surgical actions, producing temporal traces across anatomies, tools, and decisions.

This growing dataset builds a proprietary, self-reinforcing learning engine that enables a new class of software tools to augment surgical performance: surgeon-specific assistance, reusable procedural modules, predictive guidance, and next-best-step recommendations. It’s the surgical equivalent for “lane-assist,” “cruise control,” and “waypoint navigation” in autonomous driving. These tools raise the floor of clinical capability and reduce operator dependency.
