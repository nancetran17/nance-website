Rabbit Hole #3: How Do You License an AI Doctor?

You don't license an AI doctor. You give a specific AI system permission to perform a specific clinical task.

Nancy TranAug 31, 2026Read on Substack

In August 2026, the FDA’s new discussion paper broke the internet — or at least the AI healthcare crowd’s internet — by asking whether some generative-AI-enabled medical devices should be evaluated for both.

TL;DR: My answer is yes, but with a clear boundary. You do not license an AI doctor. You give a specific AI system permission to perform a specific clinical task.

The system should not be declared “doctor-level.” The finished product should be able to prove that it can perform one defined job for a defined patient population in a defined setting, with clear human oversight.

In other words, I believe that Medical AI should earn bounded clinical privileges.

Why ordinary software testing is not enough

Think about a calculator.

A calculator receives a math problem. We know the right answer because arithmetic follows fixed rules, and we can work out the result independently. Give it the same input, and it should return the same output.

That's roughly how conventional device software gets tested: write the requirements, define the expected output for each input, run representative cases and boundary cases, and check that the thing stays inside its rules. Regulators call this verification and validation.

How about a doctor?

When a doctor walks into a room, there’s usually no answer key. A patient may forget a medication, minimize chest pain, or leave out a pregnancy. Two symptoms may point in different directions. The safe recommendation can change when one new fact appears. A clinician has to know what to ask, what not to assume, and when the available information is not enough.

Some medical AI products are beginning to operate in this messier category. Ada asks patients follow-up questions and recommends how urgently they should seek care. Abridge reviews clinical context, listens to the visit, drafts documentation, and supports orders and follow-up work. Hippocratic AI’s voice agents call patients after discharge, ask about symptoms and medications, schedule appointments, document the outcome in hospital systems, and escalate warning signs to a nurse (Ada Health; Abridge; Hippocratic AI).

These products do not all diagnose, prescribe, or act independently. But they are no longer simple calculators that take a single fixed input and return a single fixed answer. They interact with patients, clinicians, medical records, and other software over time. For GenAI products, behavior may also change with the wording of a prompt, the order of information, the underlying model, or a software update.

A conventional test can only show how one version of a system behaved on a defined set of cases. It cannot establish how that system will behave across every future interaction.

This is the problem the FDA is trying to address. In August 2026, the agency published a discussion paper outlining possible approaches and 26 questions for public feedback. The paper proposes two broad stages: device benchmarking, followed by clinical confirmation. First, test what the system knows and how it behaves. Then test the complete product in the environment where it will actually be used (FDA, pp. 10–16).

I think this is the right direction.

A benchmark is not the product

Medical GenAI is tested in several ways. At the simplest level, models answer USMLE-style questions or medical question-answer datasets. Clinicians may also grade longer responses for accuracy, possible harm, bias, and helpfulness. Newer evaluations are more realistic: HealthBench uses multi-turn health conversations, while CRAFT-MD places models in simulated doctor-patient encounters where they must ask questions and gather a history. Researchers have also begun testing whether physicians perform better when using these tools.

Each step brings the test closer to clinical practice. But none, by itself, proves that the finished product is safe for real patients.

To address this gap, a 2024 Nature Medicine study tried to make the test more realistic. Instead of giving models only one-shot medical questions, researchers evaluated GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Mistral, and LLaMA-2-7b in simulated doctor-patient conversations across 12 specialties. The models still showed limitations in history-taking, conversational reasoning, and diagnostic accuracy. The authors recommended testing full conversations and open-ended questioning, not only final answers (Johri et al., Nature Medicine, 2024).

There is, however, a more fundamental problem: the model is not the product.

A hospital deploys a system made of the model, prompts, retrieval sources, interface, tools, guardrails, and clinical workflow. The same foundation model could sit underneath a note summarizer, a patient-facing triage assistant, or software that files a clinical order. Those products do not carry the same risk simply because they share a model.

Evidence for that distinction is beginning to emerge. In a randomized trial of 50 physicians, those given access to an LLM received a median diagnostic-reasoning score of 76 percent, compared with 74 percent for physicians using conventional resources. The difference was not statistically significant. Yet the LLM alone scored 92 percent on the same cases. This was a small, vignette-based study, not a trial of patient outcomes. But it shows why “the model performed well” and “the clinical workflow improved” are different claims (Goh et al., JAMA Network Open, 2024).

The clearest example is triage.

In a 2026 Nature Medicine study, researchers tested ChatGPT Health on 60 clinician-authored vignettes across 21 medical domains. Among clear emergency cases, the system under-triaged 51.6 percent, directing cases involving diabetic ketoacidosis or impending respiratory failure toward evaluation within 24 to 48 hours rather than the emergency department. It also over-triaged 64.8 percent of clearly nonurgent cases, although none of those cases were sent to the emergency department (Ramaswamy et al., Nature Medicine, 2026).

This was a controlled study, not a measurement of real-world hospital volume. But it shows why an average score is not enough. A triage product has to be evaluated where its errors matter: missed emergencies, unnecessary escalations, failure to request missing information, and inconsistent behavior when the same case is described differently.

What a clinical privilege would contain

The phrase “AI doctor” is too broad to be useful. A human physician is not authorized to do everything in medicine either. Hospitals grant privileges for specific procedures and specific levels of supervision.

I think medical AI should be treated similarly. Not because the system has human judgment or professional responsibility — it does not — but because, like hospital privileges for physicians, it should be granted only narrowly defined permissions. The structure forces us to define those permissions precisely.

A clinical privilege for an AI system should state:

  • the task it may perform;

  • the patients for whom it has been evaluated;

  • the environment and software configuration in which it may operate;

  • the actions it may recommend or take;

  • the human review it cannot bypass;

  • the conditions that require it to stop or defer; and

  • the monitoring and changes that trigger reassessment.

For example, a system might be allowed to collect symptoms and flag emergency warning signs for adults in a specific hospital network. It might not be allowed to diagnose, prescribe, or discharge a patient. A clinician might have to review every recommendation before an order is filed.

That is much more meaningful than saying the model passed a medical exam or performs at a “doctor-level.”

The FDA’s proposed competency areas support this approach. The paper asks whether the system can recognize danger, stay within its scope, apply clinical knowledge correctly, work across different patient groups, request missing information, use tools safely, and stop before taking an irreversible action (FDA, Appendix A).

These are difficult things to test. But that is partly the point. Stress-testing should expose the gap between what the product appears to do and what it can reliably do.

We have already seen why local performance matters. An external validation of Epic’s widely deployed sepsis prediction model, which examined 38,455 hospitalizations, found an area under the curve of 0.63. At the threshold studied, the model would have missed 67 percent of sepsis cases while alerting on 18 percent of all hospitalizations. This was conventional predictive AI, not GenAI, and it was a retrospective study. But it is a useful warning: a model can be widely available and still perform poorly in a specific clinical setting (Wong et al., JAMA Internal Medicine, 2021).

When a failure appears, the test should help locate it. Is the problem in the model, the prompt, the data source, the interface, or the workflow? That gives scientists and developers something concrete to improve.

The FDA also needs to make the path clear

My concern is not that the FDA will set a high standard. I want a high standard. High standards are always good — they push us to think harder and find creative ways to solve problems.

My concern is that “it depends” could become the end of the analysis, and if repeated long enough, it will invite the end of innovation — innovation that we desperately need right now in an age of increasing labor shortage and aging population. GenAI products vary by task, user, level of autonomy, and the consequences of failure. The requirements should depend on those factors. But the agency still needs to explain how each factor affects the required evidence.

If the FDA rejects a product or requests additional evidence, the company should understand what failed, why the remaining uncertainty matters, and what additional tests, design changes, or data could alter the decision. This does not mean every intended use should have a path to approval. Some may remain too risky for the available technology. But companies should be able to distinguish between a fixable evidence gap and a fundamental problem with the product.

Clear standards are useful for more than regulation. They create technical targets. If a system fails because it does not request missing information, developers can improve its information-gathering process. If it fails after a model update, the product needs better version control and reassessment. If clinicians over-rely on confident outputs, the interface or workflow needs to change.

Stress-testing can move the industry forward when the failure criteria are specific enough to guide the next round of technical work. It gives everyone a clearer definition of what “ready” means without lowering expectations or rejecting unfamiliar technology without explanation.

My Conclusion

I still do not know what threshold should define competency for every clinical task. I also do not know how often these systems should be reassessed or whether most hospitals can monitor them at the level the FDA may require. The FDA paper also leaves open the question of whether the agency’s existing authority is sufficient.

But I think the central idea is clear.

We should stop asking whether an AI model is generally capable of acting like a doctor, and instead ask whether a specific product has been granted permission to perform a specific clinical task under specific conditions and with specific limits.

That is a harder claim for a company to prove, but it is also a more useful one.
